The AI Economy: Where Will Humans Fit?
Continuous Updates · Trend Thinking Series Introduction · 3 Clues
AI is more than just technology; it is reshaping the positions of humans and work.

On one side, top Silicon Valley engineers pull in hundreds of millions in annual salaries and plan to retire before 35; on the other, white-collar workers anxiously watch AI take over their tasks.

This isn't a question of "will AI replace humans," but rather "into how many layers will AI divide humanity."
Read This First · Plain English Edition

Three straightforward takeaways:
First, AI is redistributing "who is valuable"; top engineers who can write code are more valuable, while ordinary white-collar roles are starting to be taken over by AI.
Second, this divide is global; those reaping the AI dividend are countries in the supply chain like the US, China, and South Korea, while Africa and Latin America risk being left even further behind.
Third, no one knows what this new world looks like; even the economist who wrote this piece admits they don't know if this world will tear itself apart.

Glossary of Plain English Terms:

White-collar work≈ Office jobs (coding, analysis, finance)—these are the most easily replaced by AI.

AI supply chain≈ The chain required to build AI: chips, computing power, data centers. Countries that squeeze in reap the rewards; those that don't struggle even for scraps.

UBI (Universal Basic Income)≈ The government periodically gives everyone a sum of money, regardless of employment status. A favorite topic for Silicon Valley when they get anxious.

Outsourcing Industry≈ Just as developed countries outsourced IT work to India and elsewhere, this may be the very first industry devoured by AI.

DEEP DIVE
Human roles are being reshuffled by AI
I. Winners: People who know how to direct AI are more valuable

Top Silicon Valley engineers are making hundreds of millions of dollars a year and considering retirement before 35. As Karpathy said, "My demand has expanded tenfold."AI hasn't eliminated "people who can write code," but it has made "people who can write code and direct AI" much more valuable.。

Human role shifts from "producing" to "directing":Previously humans wrote code; now they tell AI what to write and check if it's correct.。

II. The Anxiety Layer: White-collar job security is shaking

Anthropic admits Claude writes 80% of code, prompting employees to ask, "What's left for me?" Rogoff writes that "AI is devouring white-collar jobs like plankton."Office-bound middle management is the primary target for AI takeover.。

HBR data adds an ironic footnote to this anxiety: employees spend 6.4 hours a week "babysitting" AI—more time than they spend actually working with it.Humans aren't replaced by AI yet; instead, they've become AI's babysitters.Feeding context, fixing errors, and reviewing outputs.

This group isn't facing "unemployment," but a "revaluation of their role":KPMG research shows that when facing AI, 50% of people get stronger, 25% stay stagnant, and 25% actually get weaker. AI doesn't flatten the gap—it widens it.。

III. The Global Layer: Left-Behind Nations

Rogoff's observation is the most sobering:The people truly left behind by AI aren't in the US, but in countries squeezed out of the AI supply chain.Africa lacks electricity, Latin America lacks capital, and India's outsourcing industry bears the brunt.

This international research quantifies that chasm:90% % of AI computing power is concentrated in the US and China; by 2030, AI's water consumption will equal that of 13 hundred million people, with the bill footed primarily by communities hosting data centers and countries receiving e-waste.Poor nations aren't just missing out on AI wealth; they're footing the electricity and water bills for AI.。

China's situation is unique: it is both an AI powerhouse and must face the social stability issues of AI replacing jobs.The AI divide isn't just between individuals, but between nations, and between compute centers and the populations whose resources are occupied.。

IV. The Unknown: Nobody knows what this world will look like

Rogoff concludes candidly:"No one truly knows what such a world will look like, let alone how to stop it from tearing itself apart."。

This special report will be updated continuously.Every wave of talent migration, every discussion on "what humans should do", and every employment data point serves as a footnote to this question.。

5. A Deeper Layer: Humans Begin Seeking Meaning from AI

Beyond work, money, and status lies an even deeper layer:Humans are beginning to look to AI for companionship and meaning. HBR research shows that AI's top use case in 2026 will be "therapy and companionship," with a new trend being people having AI generate horoscopes and tarot readings.

Psychologists have put it most brutally:"We used to look for God in the night sky; now we stare at screens asking Claude, a pale copy of our humanity". When AI can write code, chat with people, and divine the future all at once, the issue concerning humanity is no longer just about being "replaced," but rather "what is left of us?"

On the other side is Halupedia: a fake encyclopedia fabricated by AI, drawing 150,000 users a week.Human trust in AI is simultaneously breeding both "oracles" and "hallucinations."。

6. The Counter-Wave: The AI Backlash

Not everyone welcomes AI. According to Economist statistics:40% of US voters want AI banned from most industries, and protests against data centers have shelved nearly $100 billion in projects. AI executives spent years shouting that "AI will replace you" and "AI will build superviruses," and those words have now backfired into a widespread fear of the technology.

As AI resistance spreads from the tech circle to ordinary voters, the question of humanity's place has acquired a new dimension: not "where will AI put me," but "should I let AI in at all?"。
